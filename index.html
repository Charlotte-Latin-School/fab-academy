What Fab Academy is

Fab Academy is a hands-on course in rapid prototyping. Each week students learn a new fabrication skill, such as 3D printing, laser cutting, computer-aided design, electronics design, CNC machining, molding and casting, networking, and programming. They then plan and complete a project that uses it.

The course grew out of Neil Gershenfeld's MIT class, "How to Make Almost Anything." It is taught to students at about 60 to 70 labs worldwide, called nodes, out of roughly 3,000 Fab Labs. Charlotte Latin's Fab Lab is one of them.

Students document every week of work on their own public website. The weekly projects build toward a capstone: a final project that combines the skills from the whole course.

Work is reviewed in three ways: by the local instructors, by Neil Gershenfeld, and by a global evaluator. This year students also give three oral defenses of their work.

Fab Academy's official site has the full description and the global schedule.

What makes it different

It is a real college-level course

Charlotte Latin students take the same global course as students at labs around the world. We never offer a watered-down or junior version.

A global classroom

The course is taught by Neil Gershenfeld of MIT. Local college professors and experts work alongside Charlotte Latin's Innovation and Design teachers. Students' work is reviewed by a global evaluator, and the program ends each summer with an international Fab conference. The next one is in Finland in the first week of August 2027, and students may attend if they choose.

Students build, not just study

Every week produces a physical or working result. Students make their own circuit boards, write code that runs on hardware they built, and finish with a project of their own design.

Students own their work

Students keep the intellectual property in everything they make. Companies such as Formlabs grew out of Fab Academy projects.

More than one review

Documentation emphasizes quality over quantity. Students defend their work orally three times this year instead of once.

New for 2027

Tom Dubick built a series of electronics boards with matching presentations that students work through before class begins. AI is taught as a study tool, and the program teaches students to document their work professionally using Git.

Why do it, and why not

We want families to choose Fab Academy for the right reasons. These are the honest versions of both lists.

Good reasons to do it

  • The student wants to build digital fabrication skills.
  • The student is looking for a real challenge.
  • The student wants to experience a college-level class taught by leading people in the field.
  • The student is hardworking, mature and passionate about making things.
  • Graduates report that it helps with college clubs, teams and internships, and we believe it may be the best preparation for a design or technical career.

Reasons not to do it

  • It will look good on a college application. Graduates report other benefits, but its value in admissions is uncertain.
  • The parents think it is a good idea. The student has to want it.
  • The student's best friend is taking the class.
  • The student expects it to be easy. Students will be stressed at times and will sometimes be unhappy. The question is whether they can embrace the journey.

What to consider before you agree

Time

Class meets Monday to Thursday, 3:00 to 5:30 PM, and sometimes later. Lectures are recorded in the morning and watched on Wednesday afternoons. Fridays are usually free, and there are occasional Saturdays, 9 AM to 4 PM, for group and personal work.

Plan on up to 10 to 15 more hours a week at home for lectures, research and documentation. March and April run about 35 hours a week in total, and June about 45.

Other activities

Plan to step back from spring sports, clubs and ACT review during the class. Winter sports are fine. About 20 to 25 percent of the class time is set by the group schedule, and Machine Week is the biggest group week.

The class ends with final presentations on June 7 to 11, and students should expect long weeks in the first three weeks of summer to finish their final project. That makes most June trips and international exchange programs that leave when school ends a poor fit.

Cost

The total is $5,750: $750 for the Pre-Fab class in the fall and $5,000 for Fab Academy. That includes a 3D printer, a professional soldering iron and all materials and expenses. The money pays for materials. Payment plans and scholarships are available if cost is an issue.

The Pre-Fab payment is a nonrefundable deposit. Students who already own a 3D printer should ask Tom Dubick about the Pre-Fab cost.

The fall Pre-Fab class

Pre-Fab meets once or twice a week outside the normal school day. Students assemble their own 3D printer, build soldering skills, learn Fusion 360 and advanced fabrication skills, and start documenting their work. They must also keep their grades up.

Students, parents and teachers review progress together, and families decide in December whether to continue.

If a student falls behind

A student who has not finished by June can complete the work in the fall with no penalty. The final project is chosen early and should not change after March 1 unless the instructors and lab director approve.

Support at home

Students get a 3D printer and soldering tools and can do weekend work at home. Two habits make the workload easier: learning to troubleshoot instead of waiting for a fix, and following the instructors' early advice so problems do not pile up late. Families at the information meeting raised both.

Questions families ask

Can my child do other activities during Fab Academy?

Some students manage one, but we recommend caution. The class takes about 35 hours a week in March and April and about 45 in June, and the schedule leaves little slack. Spring sports, clubs and ACT review are the hardest to combine with it, and most June trips will not work. Winter sports are fine because they end before the spring class gets busy.

About 20 to 25 percent of class time is set by the group schedule, and Machine Week is the biggest group week. Front-loading the computer-aided design and KiCad work in the fall helps, but it does not remove the spring workload. If your child has a major commitment in the spring, talk with Tom Dubick before you decide.

Does my child need experience with electronics or design?

Not necessarily. The program welcomes different technical and cultural backgrounds, and an art student can do very well. The fall Pre-Fab class builds the basics. What matters most is that the student is willing to work hard and persist when something does not work.

Can students do the work at home?

Yes. Weekend work can be done at home, and students receive a 3D printer and soldering tools.

What if my child does not finish by June?

Unfinished students can finish in the fall with no penalty.

Will this help with college admissions?

Graduates report that it helps with college clubs, teams and internships. Its value in admissions is uncertain, so we do not recommend doing it for that reason.

Who owns what my child makes?

Students own all of their intellectual property.

Do students have to go to the conference in Finland?

No. It is optional. The next Fab conference is in Finland in the first week of August 2027.

When do we have to decide?

Families decide in December, after the fall Pre-Fab class, whether to continue into the spring course.
